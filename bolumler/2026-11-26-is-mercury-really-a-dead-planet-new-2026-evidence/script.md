# Is Mercury Really a Dead Planet? New 2026 Evidence
## Script
Five days ago, a spacecraft slipped into orbit around Mercury. And it arrived at a planet that textbooks have long described as a dead, battered rock. [S1]

But is it? In 2026, three separate lines of evidence have pointed the other way. Bright streaks that may be gas leaking out of crater walls. A planet that may be shrinking faster than we thought. And hints of volcanism. Let's see what each one really tells us, and what it does not. [S4] [S7] [S10]

First, the arrival. It matters, and it did not go perfectly.

[EVIDENCE: Orbit arrival, 21 November]

On 21 November, starting at 18:24 UTC, BepiColombo entered orbit around Mercury. That is the ESA update from two days later. [S1] The brake burn began at an altitude of about six hundred and thirty five kilometres. Contact was lost during the burn, and it returned at 21:37 UTC. [S3]

Then came the problem. A software glitch put the spacecraft stack into safe mode, and the burn ended early. One source puts it at about seventy seven percent of the planned velocity change. [S3] The result is an orbit of about seventy five hours, instead of the planned fifty eight. ESA says no extra thruster burns are needed. [S1] [S3]

So it is a capture, not a finished job. The spacecraft is still stacked together, with its two orbiters and a sunshield. This is not the final science orbit. [S1] [S3]

That distinction matters for everything that follows. Because not one of the new 2026 findings comes from BepiColombo. All of them come from older data. Mostly from NASA's MESSENGER, which orbited Mercury from 2011 to 2015. [S3]

[EVIDENCE: Bright streaks, Bern]

Start with the streaks. In January 2026, a team at the University of Bern, led by Bickel, published a study in Communications Earth and Environment. They trained a machine learning tool on about one hundred and twelve thousand MESSENGER images. [S4]

It found about four hundred bright streaks. The team calls them slope lineae. [S4]

Here is the pattern. They sit on sun facing slopes of young craters. Many of them begin at hollows, the strange shallow pits that dot Mercury's surface. [S4] [S5]

The proposed explanation is volatile loss. Gas, perhaps sulfur or other light elements, escaping from the rock and leaving streaks behind. [S4] [S5]

Now the caution. About ninety three percent of the streaks are in the north, and the team ties that to how well MESSENGER imaged each region. About ten percent are not in craters at all, so other mechanisms may be at work. [S4] An outside scientist called the link to volatiles plausible. Plausible. Not proven. [S5]

And nobody has watched a streak form. [S4]

[EVIDENCE: Hollows are young]

Those hollows are a clue in their own right. NASA says they are about one hundred thousand years old on average. [S11] That sounds old. But Mercury is about four and a half billion years old. A hundred thousand years is a tiny sliver of that, around two thousandths of one percent. And NASA says hollows may still be forming. [S11]

A 2025 study in JGR Planets points the same way. It found most hollows are in an early stage, and likely still evolving. The authors say BepiColombo can look for change. [S12]

Hold that thought. We will come back to it.

[EVIDENCE: Shrinking planet, GRL]

Now the second line of evidence. Mercury is shrinking. That part is not new. As its huge metal core cooled, the planet contracted, and its crust buckled into long cliffs called thrust faults. [S9]

What is new is the size of the effect. In September 2026, Nishiyama and colleagues at the German Aerospace Center published a paper in Geophysical Research Letters. Their argument is that rough, rubble covered terrain hides these faults from view. [S7]

Earlier estimates put the total loss in Mercury's diameter at four to sixteen kilometres. The new upper estimate is about twenty three kilometres. [S7]

Twenty three kilometres is about two and a half times the height of Mount Everest, shaved off the planet. Press coverage differs on the exact figure, and some outlets quote about nineteen. So treat the number as an estimate, with a range. [S7]

There is a second question that matters even more. Is Mercury still shrinking today? In 2023, a study in Nature Geoscience, led by Man at the Open University, counted seven hundred and twenty seven small grabens. These are tiny trenches in the crust. [S8]

They estimated these features to be three hundred million years old or younger. The authors read them as a sign of ongoing contraction. [S8] [S9]

But they also said it may be refuted by BepiColombo imagery. [S9] That is an honest scientist, leaving the door open to being wrong.

[EVIDENCE: Volcanism hints, EPSC]

The third line is volcanism. Ancient volcanism on Mercury is well established, both lava flows and explosive eruptions. [S10] The open question is whether anything is active today.

In September 2026, conference abstracts at the EPSC meeting suggested possible ancient buried ash deposits near the crater Glinka. They suggested several explosive episodes at two bright patches called Agwo and Abeeso. And they described a tentative, very large cone of volcanic debris inside the Caloris basin. [S10]

Be careful here. These are conference abstracts. They are not peer reviewed papers. And they are about deposits left behind, not eruptions seen happening. [S10]

So where does that leave the label, dead planet?

Let's be exact about what we have. Landforms. Spectra. Counts of small features. All of it is indirect. No eruption has been observed on Mercury. No new fault scarp. No new streak. [S4] [S10]

So the evidence does not prove Mercury is alive. What it does is put pressure on the word dead. The better phrase may be probably still active, and we are about to test that. [S4] [S8]

There is one more puzzle that shows why this is hard. ESA lists three oddities of Mercury. An inner core that seems too large. Surprising chemistry. And water ice, on a world where surface temperatures can reach four hundred and fifty degrees Celsius. [S2]

Ice survives in permanently shadowed craters near the poles. [S2] It is a planet of extremes, and it keeps refusing to fit a simple story.

[EVIDENCE: BepiColombo roadmap]

So what happens next? Remember that BepiColombo is not finished. ESA's schedule runs like this. On 9 and 10 December, the Japanese orbiter Mio separates from the European one, the MPO. On 16 December the sunshield is released. [S1]

The MPO should reach its final orbit around 10 March 2027, and the science phase starts in April 2027. ESA says these dates may change. [S1]

The journey so far has already taken about eight years. BepiColombo launched on 20 October 2018. [S3] It is the first European and Japanese mission to Mercury. It is the first to carry two orbiters there. And it is only the second mission ever to orbit the planet. [S2]

The MPO carries eleven instruments. They include a laser altimeter, a magnetometer, a thermal infrared spectrometer and a high resolution camera system. [S3] Mio, from Japan's space agency, studies the magnetic field, the plasma around the planet, and its thin sodium atmosphere. [S3]

On 6 October, first light tests of some instruments, including the laser altimeter and the imaging system, were done. [S1]

Now think about what these tools can answer. A laser altimeter can measure whether a fault scarp has moved. A camera can look for new streaks and changing hollows, where MESSENGER only gave us one frozen era. Magnetometers can probe the core. [S3] [S12]

Here is why that matters. MESSENGER gave us a snapshot. Four years of images that were then used to build a map. BepiColombo is built to compare. To look at the same place again, and again, and see whether anything has changed. [S12]

That is how the question about a dead planet gets settled. Not by another clever analysis of old pictures, but by watching.

And it is a fair bet that the first answer will be less dramatic than a headline. Probably small changes. Slow ones. A hollow that grew slightly. A streak that appears over months. The kind of change that looks like nothing, until you realise nothing was supposed to happen at all.

So is Mercury a dead planet? Based on what we can say today, it is more likely a quiet one. A world that cooled, contracted, and may still be letting go of its gas, its heat and its crust. The honest answer is that we do not know yet. And for the first time in over a decade, we have a spacecraft in position to find out.
