# Is Mercury Really a Dead Planet? New 2026 Evidence
## Script
Five days ago, a spacecraft slipped into orbit around Mercury. It arrived at a planet often thought of as a dead, battered rock. But is it? [S1]

In 2026, new studies added to a decade of hints from NASA's MESSENGER mission that Mercury may not be fully dead. Only one of them speaks directly to recent activity. The others refine the planet's past. Let's see what each one tells us, and what it does not. [S4] [S6] [S9]

First, the arrival. It matters, and it did not go perfectly.

[EVIDENCE: Orbit arrival, 21 November]

BepiColombo entered orbit around Mercury on 21 November. [S1] A software glitch put the spacecraft into safe mode, and the braking burn ended early. [S1] [S3] So the orbit is longer than planned, about seventy five hours instead of fifty eight. ESA says no extra thruster burns are needed. [S1] [S3]

It is a capture, not a finished job. This is not the final science orbit. [S1]

And that matters for everything that follows. Not one of the new 2026 findings comes from BepiColombo. They come from older data, mostly from MESSENGER, which orbited Mercury from 2011 to 2015. [S12]

[EVIDENCE: Bright streaks, Bern]

Start with the streak study, the one that speaks most directly to recent activity. In early 2026, a team at the University of Bern trained a machine learning tool on about one hundred and twelve thousand MESSENGER images. [S4]

It found about four hundred bright streaks. They sit on sun facing slopes of young craters, and many begin at hollows, the shallow pits that dot Mercury's surface. [S4] [S5]

The proposed explanation is volatile loss. Gas, perhaps sulfur or other light elements, escaping from the rock and leaving streaks behind. [S4] [S5]

Now the caution. About ninety three percent of the streaks are in the north, and the team ties that to how well MESSENGER imaged each region. About ten percent are not in craters at all, so other mechanisms may be at work. [S4] An outside scientist called the link to volatiles plausible. Plausible. Not proven. [S5]

And nobody has watched a streak form. [S4]

[EVIDENCE: Hollows are young]

Those hollows are a clue in their own right, and they are not new. MESSENGER found them back in 2011. What is newer is the question of how old they are. [S10] [S11]

NASA says some estimates put them at roughly a hundred thousand years, on average. The dating is uncertain, but it is very young by geological standards. [S10] Against four and a half billion years, that is a blink. If Mercury's life were one year, the hollows formed in the last few minutes. And NASA says they may still be forming. [S10]

A 2025 study agrees. It found most hollows are in an early stage, and likely still evolving. The authors say BepiColombo can look for change. [S11]

Keep the hollows in mind. We will come back to them.

[EVIDENCE: Shrinking planet, GRL]

Now the second study. Mercury is shrinking. That part is not new. As the planet cooled, its crust buckled into long cliffs called thrust faults. [S8]

What is new is how much. In September 2026, researchers at the German Aerospace Center argued that rough, rubble covered terrain hides many of these faults from view. [S6]

Earlier estimates put the total loss in Mercury's diameter at four to sixteen kilometres. The new upper estimate is roughly twenty kilometres, and outlets quote anywhere from nineteen to twenty three. [S6] That is about two Everests, not removed, but squeezed, as the crust folded over itself. [S6]

Notice what this study is. It is about how much Mercury has shrunk over its whole history. It does not measure how fast it shrinks today. So by itself, it does not tell us the planet is active now. [S6]

That question belongs to an older study. In 2023, researchers at the Open University counted seven hundred and twenty seven small trenches in the crust, called grabens. [S7]

They estimated these features to be three hundred million years old or younger, and read them as a sign of ongoing contraction. [S7] [S8]

But the team also said that this reading may be refuted by BepiColombo imagery. [S8] That is an honest scientist, leaving the door open to being wrong.

[EVIDENCE: Volcanism hints, EPSC]

The third study area is volcanism. Ancient volcanism on Mercury is widely accepted. The open question is whether anything is active today.

In September 2026, conference abstracts at the EPSC meeting suggested possible buried ash deposits near the crater Glinka. They suggested several explosive episodes at two bright patches called Agwo and Abeeso. And they described a tentative, very large cone of volcanic debris inside the Caloris basin. [S9]

Be careful here. These are conference abstracts. They are not peer reviewed papers. And they describe deposits left behind, not eruptions seen happening. [S9]

So where does that leave the label, dead planet?

Let's be exact. Landforms. Spectra. Counts of small features. All of it is indirect. No eruption has been observed on Mercury. No new fault scarp. No new streak. [S4] [S9]

So the evidence does not prove Mercury is alive. What it does is put pressure on the word dead. The better word may be quiet. And quiet is not the same as finished. [S4] [S7]

It is a planet of extremes. ESA notes water ice on a world where surface temperatures can reach four hundred and fifty degrees Celsius. [S2] Mercury keeps refusing to fit a simple story.

[EVIDENCE: BepiColombo roadmap]

So how do we settle it? Remember that BepiColombo is not finished. ESA says the science phase should start in April 2027, though dates may change. [S1]

The craft is a European and Japanese mission, and only the second ever to orbit Mercury. It launched in 2018, so this arrival took about eight years. [S2] [S3]

Now think about what its tools can add. The European orbiter carries a laser altimeter. It cannot watch a fault slip, because over a few years that motion would be far too small. But it can map fault cliffs in far more detail, sharpening how much Mercury has really shrunk. [S3]

It also carries a high resolution camera system. That one can look for fresh features that cut across young terrain. [S3]

And here is the callback. Remember the hollows, young and maybe still forming? This is the instrument that could catch one changing. [S11]

The key comparison is MESSENGER, from 2011 to 2015, against BepiColombo, more than a decade later. A second, sharper look. Comparing the two is how we can search for change. [S11] [S12]

My guess is that if anything changes, it will be small and slow. A hollow edge that moved. A streak that was not there before. But it is entirely possible we will see no change at all. And that would also be an answer.

So is Mercury a dead planet? Based on what we can say today, it is more likely a quiet one. A world that cooled, contracted, and may still be losing its gas and its heat, and slowly tightening its crust. The honest answer is that we do not know yet. And for the first time in over a decade, we have a spacecraft in position to find out.
