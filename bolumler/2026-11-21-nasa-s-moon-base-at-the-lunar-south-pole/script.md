# NASA Wants a Moon Base. Here Is What Is Actually Real.
## Script
NASA now calls it a Moon Base. Not a landing. Not a flag and footprints mission. A base. [S1]

So why has nobody landed a single piece of it yet? [S1]

And why did the agency throw away its space station around the Moon to chase it? [S5]

Let's separate what is real from what is a drawing.

Start with the pivot. In March 2026, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman paused the Gateway station, a planned outpost in lunar orbit. The goal was to focus on a base on the surface instead. [S5]

[EVIDENCE: Gateway paused, not cancelled]

Notice the word paused. NASA says Gateway is not cancelled. But its hardware may be repurposed. [S5]

That matters, because a lot of the headlines made it sound final. It is not. It is a change of priorities, and the partners who were building pieces of Gateway are still waiting to learn what happens to them. [S5]

Now, the word base. Picture a city under a dome. That is not what NASA is describing, at least not yet. [S2]

[EVIDENCE: Three phases, 2029 and 2032 marks]

The plan has three phases. Phase One runs from now to 2029. NASA calls it Gain Reliable Access, Experiment, and Learn. In plain words, robots, tech demos, and scouting. [S2]

Phase Two, from 2029 to 2032, is Build and Expand. Early power, cargo, logistics, and communications. [S2]

Only Phase Three, from 2032 onward, is Live and Work on the Moon. That is the permanent outpost, with habitats, power, comms, and transport. [S2]

So today, the Moon Base is a schedule of robotic missions. Not a building. [S2]

But why build it at the south pole? Of all the places on the Moon, why there? [S1]

Here is the first reason. Water. [S8]

[EVIDENCE: Ice in permanently shadowed craters]

Parts of the polar craters never see sunlight. These are called permanently shadowed regions. They can reach about minus two hundred fifty degrees Celsius. That is colder than the surface of Pluto. [S4]

In that cold, water ice can survive for a very long time. One radar based estimate puts it at more than six hundred billion kilograms. That is about two hundred forty thousand Olympic swimming pools. [S4]

But hold on. That is a low end estimate. And it does not mean lakes. This is ice mixed into soil, and nobody knows exactly how much there is, or what form it takes. [S4]

Here is another thing to question. Where did it come from? Comets, asteroids, the solar wind, even Earth. Scientists have not settled that. [S4]

[EVIDENCE: LCROSS measured only 155 kilograms]

The best direct evidence is from 2009. NASA's LCROSS probe hit a crater and detected about one hundred fifty five kilograms of water. A few bathtubs worth, from one impact. [S4]

And India's Chandrayaan-3 measured temperatures at high latitudes. It did not directly detect ice. [S8]

So the promise of water is real, but the proof is thin. That is exactly why the first missions are scouting missions. [S4]

There is a second reason for the pole. Light. The popular idea of a sunless pole is wrong. Some ridges there get near continuous sunlight, while the shadowed craters sit nearby. [S4]

Sunlight for power, and ice in the dark. Close together. That is the pitch. [S1]

Now, who is actually going? NASA numbers the missions. [S3]

[EVIDENCE: Moon Base I, II, and III landers]

Moon Base One is Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mark One lander, named Endurance, aimed at the Shackleton Connecting Ridge. [S3]

Moon Base Two is Astrobotic's Griffin, carrying more than eleven hundred pounds of cargo, including Astrolab's FLIP rover. [S3]

Moon Base Three is Intuitive Machines' Nova-C lander, called Trinity, carrying an instrument named Lunar Vertex. [S3]

Notice something. All three are commercial landers. NASA is buying rides, not building every machine itself. [S3]

And those rides have slipped. Reports say a New Glenn rocket exploded on its pad in May 2026. The first Blue Moon and Nova-C launches then moved from fall 2026 to the first part of 2027. That detail comes from a summary of a trade report, so treat it with care. [S11]

As of the sources I found, no Moon Base lander has touched down yet. I could not confirm that either way, so check the latest news. [S1]

What comes after the landers? Rovers. And a few strange machines. [S2]

[EVIDENCE: PROMISE nuclear rover, MoonFall hoppers]

PROMISE is a six wheeled, nuclear powered rover. It carries a drill that reaches about one meter down, and it is designed for an eighteen month mission. [S2]

On November seventh, NASA announced awards at the MAX POWER expo at Kennedy Space Center. Blue Origin received a ninety four million dollar base contract, plus a one hundred forty million dollar option, to deliver PROMISE. The target is late 2028. [S9]

Then there is MoonFall. Four hopping drones from JPL, planned for 2028, meant to map landing sites. They fly across a lunar day, which lasts about fourteen Earth days. [S2]

VIPER, the water hunting rover, is slated for late 2027 on a second Blue Moon lander. [S2]

For people, NASA plans two crewed capable rovers by 2028. They are unpressurized, so astronauts ride in their suits. Astrolab and Lunar Outpost won the contracts, each worth about two hundred twenty million dollars. [S2] [S3]

[EVIDENCE: Pressurized rover, 28 day missions]

Later comes the part that feels like a base. A pressurized rover from Japan's space agency. A mobile lab where crews can work without spacesuits, on missions of up to twenty eight days. [S2]

One lunar day is about fourteen days of sunlight, then fourteen of night. Surviving that night is the real engineering problem. [S2]

Which brings us to power. [S6]

[EVIDENCE: 100 kilowatt reactor target]

NASA is aiming for a fission reactor of about one hundred kilowatts on the surface by around 2030. That is roughly the power of an office building, according to an expert quoted in coverage. [S6]

Experts doubt the timeline, more than the size. A small twenty kilowatt test reactor is part of the effort, but a working surface reactor by 2030 is a steep climb. [S6]

And that is the honest tension at the center of this story. Can the schedule hold? [S6]

[EVIDENCE: Experts doubt 2027 and 2028 dates]

Experts quoted in coverage question the 2027 and 2028 landing dates. The Artemis crewed lander still needs development. And NASA itself notes steep slopes, ridges, and deep craters at the pole. Add dust and extreme cold, and even a perfect landing is hard to turn into mining. [S6] [S10]

So the rebel question is this. Is the Moon Base a plan, or a promise written on a slide? [S6]

Part of the answer is that some of it is already real. Artemis II sent a crew around the Moon. NASA's page highlights images from the April sixth flyby, including the Moon passing in front of the Sun. The crew did not land. [S1]

Money is moving too. In late May, NASA announced Lunar Terrain Vehicle awards. And Blue Origin was given one hundred eighty eight million dollars to deliver the rovers, with a two hundred eighty million dollar option. [S3]

[EVIDENCE: China delays Chang'e-7]

Now the race. China is also aiming for the south pole. Its Chang'e-7 mission, built to look for water ice near Shackleton, was postponed in August to 2027. [S7]

China says it plans a crewed landing by around 2030. It is also planning an International Lunar Research Station with Russia, with construction from about 2031. Those dates are not firm, on either side. [S7]

So this is not a clean sprint. Two programs, both with slipping calendars, aiming at the same few square kilometers of ridge and crater. [S7]

Why does that matter? Because the best sunlit spots and the best ice are few. Whoever works there first learns the most. That is a reason for speed, and a reason for caution. [S1] [S4]

One more thing keeps the story open. NASA is inviting the public in. On the same November seventh, it announced a one million dollar Moon Base Challenge for students. Registration closes January twenty fifth, 2027. [S9]

[EVIDENCE: Student challenge, one million dollars]

That tells you something. A base is not only rockets and reactors. It is a long project that needs people who are in school right now. Phase Three, in 2032 and beyond, belongs to them. [S2] [S9]

So let's go back to the opening question. Why has nothing landed yet? [S1]

Because the base is not a place. It is a sequence. Landers first, then rovers, then power, then habitats. Each step depends on the one before it. [S2]

And why trade a space station for it? Because a station in orbit cannot drill into ice. A surface base might. That is the bet. It is still a bet. [S5] [S4]

Here is what I would watch. First, whether the first landers reach the surface in 2027. Second, whether the reactor stays near 2030. Third, whether anyone confirms ice in a form that can actually be used. [S11] [S6] [S4]

If those three hold, the drawings start to become hardware. If they do not, the plan will change again. [S2]

Either way, the south pole is where the next chapter of the Moon will be written. And we will keep tracking it, one piece of evidence at a time.
