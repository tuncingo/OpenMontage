# NASA Wants a Moon Base. Here Is What Is Actually Real.
## Script
NASA says it is building a base on the Moon. As far as I can find, not one piece of it has landed yet. [S1]

And to chase it, the agency put its own planned space station around the Moon on hold. [S5]

So what is real here, and what is just a drawing?

Start with the pivot. In March 2026, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman paused Gateway, a planned outpost in lunar orbit. The goal was to focus on a base on the surface. Press reports put the cost near twenty billion dollars. That figure is not an audited NASA number. [S5] [S12]

[EVIDENCE: Gateway paused, not cancelled]

Notice the word paused. NASA says Gateway is not cancelled, but its hardware may be repurposed. Gateway was a staging point, not a mine. NASA decided its money does more on the surface. According to one opinion piece, the fate of partners like Europe, Japan and Canada is still an open question. [S5] [S13]

Now, the word base. Picture a city under a dome. That is not what NASA describes, at least not yet. [S2]

[EVIDENCE: Three phases, 2029 and 2032 marks]

There are three phases. Phase One, now to 2029, is robots, tech demos and scouting. Phase Two, 2029 to 2032, adds early power, cargo and communications. Only Phase Three, from 2032, is the permanent outpost with habitats. [S2]

So today, the Moon Base is a schedule of robotic missions. Not a building. [S2]

But why the south pole? Of all the places on the Moon? [S1]

The first reason is water. [S4]

[EVIDENCE: Ice in permanently shadowed craters]

Parts of the polar craters never see sunlight. They can reach about minus two hundred fifty degrees Celsius. That is colder than the surface of Pluto. In that cold, water ice can survive for a very long time. [S4]

One radar based estimate for the polar regions puts it at more than six hundred billion kilograms. That is about two hundred forty thousand Olympic swimming pools. But it is a low end figure for the poles as a whole, not a number for the base site. And it does not mean lakes. This is ice mixed into soil. [S4]

[EVIDENCE: LCROSS, the first direct sample]

The first direct sample came in 2009. NASA's LCROSS probe hit a crater and kicked up a plume containing about one hundred fifty five kilograms of water. That is only what the plume let us see. It is not a total. Other instruments, including Chandrayaan-1 and the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, also point to polar ice. [S4]

Chandrayaan-3 measured temperatures at high latitudes, but it did not directly detect ice. [S8]

So the evidence that ice exists is solid. What is thin is how much there is, what form it takes, and whether anyone can mine it. [S4]

And here is the loop I cannot close. Where did it come from? Comets, asteroids, the solar wind, even Earth. Scientists have not settled that. Hold on to that question. [S4]

There is a second reason for the pole. Light. The idea of a sunless pole is wrong. Some ridges there get near continuous sunlight, while the shadowed craters sit nearby. [S8]

Sunlight for power, ice in the dark. Close together. That is the pitch. [S1]

Now, who is actually going? NASA numbers the missions. [S3]

[EVIDENCE: Three commercial landers]

Three commercial landers, from Blue Origin, Astrobotic and Intuitive Machines. The first is aimed at the Shackleton Connecting Ridge. NASA is buying rides, not building every machine itself. [S3]

Those rides have slipped. One trade report says a New Glenn rocket exploded on its pad in May, pushing the first landers to early 2027. I could not confirm that independently, so check the latest news. [S11]

What comes after the landers? Two strange machines. [S2]

[EVIDENCE: PROMISE nuclear rover]

First, PROMISE. A six wheeled rover powered by a nuclear source. It carries a drill that reaches about one meter down, and it is designed for an eighteen month mission. Blue Origin won a ninety four million dollar contract to deliver it, with a target of late 2028. [S2] [S9]

[EVIDENCE: MoonFall hopping drones]

Second, MoonFall. Four hopping drones from JPL, planned for 2028. Not wheels. Hops. Their job is to map landing sites, flying across a lunar day of about fourteen Earth days. Why hop? That is a design choice I would love to hear the engineers explain. [S2]

Later comes the part that feels like a base. A pressurized rover from Japan's space agency. A mobile lab where crews work without spacesuits, on missions of up to twenty eight days. [S2]

Which brings us to power. [S6]

A lunar day is about fourteen Earth days of sunlight, then fourteen of night. The polar ridges are an exception, with near continuous sun. But the crater floors never see light. Power through darkness is the hard problem. In my view, that is why NASA wants a reactor. [S2] [S8]

[EVIDENCE: 100 kilowatt reactor target]

According to reports, NASA is aiming for a fission reactor of about one hundred kilowatts on the surface by around 2030. That is roughly the power of an office building, says an expert quoted in coverage. [S6]

Experts doubt the timeline more than the size. A working surface reactor by 2030 is a steep climb. [S6]

And that is the honest tension at the center of this story. Can the schedule hold? [S6]

[EVIDENCE: Experts doubt 2027 and 2028 dates]

Experts question the 2027 and 2028 landing dates. The Artemis crewed lander still needs development. [S6]

Reports say the first crewed landing may now fall to Artemis IV, tentatively 2028. Sources conflict. The crew rovers depend on that date. [S14]

The pole has steep slopes, ridges and deep craters. Add dust and extreme cold. Even a perfect landing is not yet mining. [S10]

So the rebel question is this. Is the Moon Base a plan, or a promise on a slide? [S6]

Part of it is already real. Artemis II sent a crew around the Moon. NASA highlights images from the April sixth flyby, including the Moon passing in front of the Sun. The crew did not land. [S1]

[EVIDENCE: China delays Chang'e-7]

Now the race. China is also aiming for the south pole. Its Chang'e-7 mission, built to look for water ice near Shackleton, was postponed in August to 2027. [S7]

China says it plans a crewed landing by around 2030. It is also planning a lunar research station with Russia, with construction from about 2031. Those dates are not firm, on either side. [S7]

So this is not a clean sprint. Two programs, both with slipping calendars, both eyeing the Shackleton area. That is a reason for speed, and a reason for caution. [S3] [S7]

[EVIDENCE: Student challenge, one million dollars]

NASA is even inviting students in, with a one million dollar Moon Base Challenge. A base is a long project. Phase Three, in 2032 and beyond, belongs to people who are in school right now. [S2] [S9]

So let's go back to the opening. Why has so little been built? [S1]

Because the base is not a place. It is a sequence. Landers first, then rovers, then power, then habitats. Each step depends on the one before it. [S2]

And why shelve a space station? Gateway was a staging point. NASA bet the money does more on the surface, near the ice. Critics may argue an orbital hub still has value. It is a bet, and it is still a bet. [S5]

Here is what I would watch. Whether the first landers reach the surface, keeping in mind the early 2027 date is one unconfirmed report. Whether the reactor stays near 2030. And whether anyone can show that the ice is usable, and where it came from. [S11] [S6] [S4]

The base is still a drawing. The next two years decide whether it becomes hardware. And either way, the south pole is where both NASA and China are placing their bets. [S2] [S7]
