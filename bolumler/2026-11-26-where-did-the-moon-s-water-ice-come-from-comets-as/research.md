# Research: Where did the Moon's water ice come from?
Date of research: 2026-11-26. Note: colorado.edu and uaf.edu were not reachable from the session (network allowlist), so those items rest on search-result summaries and mirrors, not full-page reads. Only the arXiv review was fetched in full. Verify before publishing.

## Key facts
- Water ice sits in polar cold traps, permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) at roughly 110 K or colder. The Moon's tiny axial tilt keeps polar crater floors dark. All cold traps lie within PSRs, but not every PSR is a cold trap [arxiv.org/html/2502.06056v2; Diviner review via connectsci.au]. One estimate puts cold traps at about 40,000 km2, roughly 60 percent in the south [connectsci.au/news/news-parent/9149].
- Proven: LCROSS (2009) saw about 5.6 +/- 2.9 percent H2O by mass in the plume from Cabeus. Pure ice crystals were seen in the ejecta [arxiv.org/html/2502.06056v2; connectsci.au]. LRO neutron data show excess hydrogen of about 1.5 wt percent water-equivalent [arxiv.org/html/2502.06056v2].
- Candidate sources in the 2025 review "Current Theories of Lunar Ice" [https://arxiv.org/html/2502.06056v2]:
  1. Comets and hydrated asteroids. Fast comet impacts retain less water than slower asteroid impacts. The review says comets are now thought a minor source and hydrated asteroids and meteoroids supply and retain more.
  2. Volcanic outgassing. Volcanism peaked about 4 to 2 billion years ago. The review calls outgassing and impacts the largest sources of polar ice, both peaking early.
  3. Solar wind. Proton flux at 1 AU is about 2x10^12 per m2 per s. The review judges it a negligible contributor to polar ice on the Moon (about 10 percent of Mercury's), because making molecular water needs about 400 K and polar cold traps are far colder. Chang'e-5 samples show interior OH is largely solar-wind derived, but that water is not released as ice. This is a contested judgment, not settled.
  4. Dielectric breakdown by solar energetic particles in PSRs (Huang et al. 2021). Proposed, not established.
- Volcanic case (Needham and Kring, 2017): NASA slides estimate that if about 0.1 percent of water vented during mare eruptions was trapped in PSRs, it could account for today's ice. This is an estimate, not a confirmed result [ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180005682/downloads/20180005682.pdf].
- Comet case (Mandt et al., Nature Communications 2022): elemental analysis of the LCROSS plume says the volatiles are not volcanic and are most likely cometary. Volcanic and chondritic sources give the right sulfur but not enough hydrogen and nitrogen [link.springer.com/10.1038/s41467-022-28289-6; jhuapl.edu release 220208]. This conflicts with the 2025 review's "comets negligible" view.
- Earth case (Terada et al., Nature Astronomy 2017, Kaguya data): oxygen ions (O+) seen only while the Moon is in Earth's plasma sheet, so biogenic terrestrial oxygen has been blown to the Moon by magnetospheric wind [ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017NatAs...1E..26T]. UAF (Poppe/Hurley-era work, 2022 release) proposed that Earth-escaped H and O ions, hitting the Moon during the roughly 5 days per orbit it crosses the magnetotail, could form water, with an upper estimate of 3,500 km3 or more of polar permafrost or subsurface water [uaf.edu/news/archives/2022/earths-atmosphere-may-be-source-of-some-lunar-water.php]. This is a speculative upper bound; mainstream view still favors other sources. Berkeley SSL separately reported evidence that Earth's magnetosphere can create water on the lunar surface [ssl.berkeley.edu/first-evidence-that-water-can-be-created-on-the-lunar-surface-by-earths-magnetosphere].
- The Moon's interior is wetter than once thought, so the old "dry Moon from the giant impact" idea is weakened. Related: an EPSC 2024 abstract proposes magma-ocean crystallization could supply water; it is unpublished-style and untested [meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EPSC2024/EPSC2024-903.html].

## Latest developments (with dates)
- 7 Apr 2026, Nature Astronomy (Aharonson, Weizmann, with Hayne, CU Boulder): ice accumulated slowly over about 3 to 3.5 billion years, not in one giant event (a single huge comet is ruled out). The oldest, darkest cold traps hold the most ice (LAMP and LRO temperature data plus models of supply, evaporation and impact gardening). More than 10 percent of the surface of the most ancient cold traps is covered by ice. Shackleton has been shadowed about 3.5 Gyr but became a cold trap only about 500 Myr ago, so it may be a weaker target. Haworth is flagged as a priority. The study narrows but does not name the source [phys.org/news/2026-04-moon-narrows.html; colorado.edu/today/2026/04/07/water-moon-new-study-narrows-down-most-likely-locations; wis-wander.weizmann.ac.il/node/12358]. Reported secondhand.
- Late Mar to 7 Apr 2026, Univ. of Hawaii-led ShadowCam study (Science Advances per Scientific American coverage): no widespread ice above about 20 to 30 wt percent (the detection limit). Small isolated deposits of 20 to 50 m seen, many near young craters, and some signatures above 10 percent. Needs instruments sensitive below 1 percent [hawaii.edu/news/2026/04/07/moon-less-surface-ice/; earthsky.org/space/ice-in-moons-shadows-shadowcam-moon/]. Search summary said the instrument flies on the Korea Pathfinder Lunar Orbiter (KPLO/Danuri); that is correct.
- Around 10 Nov 2026, AGU Advances: craters under about 100 m wide vanish within about 100 Myr, so ice inside small craters must be recent, not ancient [techtimes.com/articles/330380/20261110/...]. Reported secondhand by a weak outlet; verify in AGU Advances.
- 23-24 Aug 2026: China postponed the Chang'e-7 launch (Long March 5, Wenchang, had been set for 24 Aug). No replacement date was confirmed in my searches; some outlets say 2027, and the Typhoon Narra link is unconfirmed. Chang'e-7 is aimed at south-pole water ice [scientificamerican.com/article/china-delays-ambitious-change-7-moon-mission-until-2027; sciencetimes.com/articles/62446/20260824/...]. Check CNSA for current status.
- NASA VIPER status is unclear (reports of cancellation, then Blue Origin selected for a 2027 delivery); Artemis III timing is inconsistent across sources. Do not state dates without checking nasa.gov.
- April 2025: Chandrayaan-3 ChaSTE data at about 69 S showed a Sun-facing 6 degree slope hit 82 C and fell to -168 C at night, better than LRO Diviner estimates [eos.org/articles/lunar-ice-might-be-easier-to-reach-than-we-thought].

## Competing hypotheses / open questions
- Comets vs hydrated asteroids vs volcanoes: which dominates is unresolved. Mandt 2022 says LCROSS looks cometary; the 2025 review says comets are minor and outgassing plus impacts are largest.
- Solar wind: strong for the thin hydrated skin of regolith (Chang'e-5 grains, 1,116 to 2,516 ppm H, very low delta-D, about 46 ppm bulk solar-wind water [PNAS 2022, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9907113]), but doubtful as a source of polar ice itself. The mechanism needs warmth, the ice needs cold. Hydrogen from the wind could migrate to poles; debated.
- Earth as source: an exotic hypothesis. Quantities are upper bounds only.
- Transport: can water molecules hop to the poles intact? Observed exosphere densities conflict with models [arxiv.org/html/2502.06056v2].
- Age of the traps: the Moon's spin axis and tilt have changed, so today's shadowed craters were not always shadowed (April 2026 paper). Ice age and source may differ by crater.
- Isotopes: polar ice D/H has not been measured directly. It would separate comet, asteroid and solar sources [airspacemag.com/daily-planet/water-from-the-sun-77291080/ says certainty needs actual ice analysis]. Future landers (Chang'e-7, VIPER-class) could test this.
- Mixed supply is likely: several sources, each dominating in different eras and places. Say this honestly in the script.

## Numbers & scale comparisons (make big numbers intuitive)
- Solar wind: about 1.3 protons per nm2 per lunation versus about 10 H2O per nm2 in a monolayer. So, roughly 8 months of solar wind would be needed to make one layer, if every proton became water [arxiv.org/html/2502.06056v2].
- Cold traps about 40,000 km2, a bit under the area of Switzerland (41,285 km2, my comparison). Ice covers just over 10 percent of the oldest ones.
- 3 to 3.5 billion years of accumulation: about 75 percent of the age of the Earth-Moon system (about 4.5 Gyr).
- Cold-trap threshold about 110 K (-163 C). ChaSTE: a 250 C day-night swing at 69 S (82 C to -168 C).
- LCROSS: 5.6 percent water is about 56 liters per tonne of dirt, drier than most terrestrial desert soils would feel to touch, but huge for the Moon (my arithmetic).
- Arnold's old estimate: 1 to 10 percent ice in the top 2 m, from impact mixing over about 2 Gyr (not updated) [arxiv.org/html/2502.06056v2].
- Volcanic: trapping only 0.1 percent of vented water would be enough (NASA slides). UAF Earth-water upper bound: 3,500 km3, about 3,500 billion tonnes, which is a deliberately large ceiling.

## Common misconceptions to avoid
- "The ice is a big frozen lake or sheet." ShadowCam finds scarce surface ice, small patches, and low concentrations. It is mixed into regolith.
- "Ice came from one giant comet." The April 2026 study argues against a single event.
- "Solar wind obviously made the ice." It makes hydroxyl/water in the dust; whether it feeds polar ice is disputed.
- "Moon was bone dry after the giant impact." Samples and orbital data show water in the interior (Chang'e-5, Apollo glass).
- "Shackleton is the best ice site." The 2026 study suggests it only recently became a cold trap.
- "Chang'e-7 launched in August." It was postponed.
- "LCROSS proved the source." It proved ice exists and gave composition; origin is still argued.
- Earth-origin water: do not present as established.

## Real imagery that exists (NASA/ESA/ESO missions, instruments, image names)
- LCROSS plume/Cabeus impact (2009) and the Centaur impact video; NASA Ames.
- LRO: LAMP (UV ice detection), Diviner (temperature maps of cold traps), LOLA (topography), LEND (neutron hydrogen).
- ShadowCam (NASA instrument on KPLO/Danuri): images inside PSRs, including Shackleton, Haworth and Cabeus; ASU/shadowcam.asu.edu.
- Chandrayaan-3 Vikram lander and ChaSTE probe (ISRO); Chandrayaan-1 M3 (NASA instrument, 2009 hydration detection).
- Chang'e-5 returned samples (CNSA) with glass beads; Apollo glass beads imagery.
- Kaguya (JAXA) plasma/ion data in the Earth's plasma sheet (Terada 2017); NASA Moon-in-Earth-magnetotail artist concepts.
- Mini-SAR/Chandrayaan-1 radar craters at the north pole.
- Earth-Moon South Pole mosaic from LRO is the best "ice map" visual. Use official archive images, label AI renders as such.
