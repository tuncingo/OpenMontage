# Where Did the Moon's Water Ice Come From?
## Script
The Moon has ice. That part is settled. What nobody can say for sure is whose water it is. [S1]

Five suspects are on the table. Comets. Asteroids. The Moon's own volcanoes. The solar wind. And, strangest of all, our own planet. [S1][S10]

One measurement could settle the case, and nobody has made it yet. We will get to it. And we will look at a lander built to attempt it, whose launch was postponed this August. [S18]

First, the scene of the crime.

In 2009, NASA's LCROSS probe crashed a spent rocket stage into Cabeus, a crater near the Moon's south pole. [S1][S16]

[EVIDENCE: LCROSS plume]

The plume was roughly five percent water by mass, with a wide margin of error. Pure ice crystals were seen in the debris. [S1][S16]

Why was there ice in there at all? The Moon barely tilts on its axis. So the floors of some polar craters never see the Sun. [S1] Many of these shadows are cold enough to trap ice, at about one hundred and ten kelvin or colder. That is minus one hundred and sixty three degrees Celsius. [S1] They are called cold traps. One estimate puts them at about forty thousand square kilometres. That is a bit less than Switzerland. [S16]

So the ice is real. The question is how it got there.

Suspect one. Comets.

A comet is a dirty snowball. It looks like the obvious answer. And in 2022, a team analysed the chemical fingerprint of the LCROSS plume. [S9]

[EVIDENCE: LCROSS chemical fingerprint]

They compared it with volcanoes, and with asteroid-like material. Neither matched well. Comets did. [S9]

That sounds like a case closed. But it is not.

A 2025 review of lunar ice theories sees it differently. Comets hit the Moon very fast, and a fast impact keeps less water. So the review treats comets as a minor source. [S1]

Remember that disagreement. These are two serious lines of evidence pointing different ways. One is a chemical fingerprint from a single impact at a single crater. The other is impact physics applied to the whole Moon. And Cabeus may not speak for every cold trap. [S1][S9]

Suspect two. Asteroids.

Slower hits by hydrated asteroids and meteoroids can deliver water and hold on to more of it. [S1] That is why the review leans on them, while the fingerprint study found asteroid-like material a poor match. [S1][S9] Same suspect, two verdicts.

Suspect three. The Moon itself.

Volcanism on the Moon peaked early, roughly four to two billion years ago. Eruptions vented gas, and some of it was water. [S1] The review calls outgassing and impacts the largest sources of polar ice. [S1]

[EVIDENCE: volcanic trapping estimate]

How much would it take? According to a NASA slide deck, if only about one tenth of one percent of the water vented in the big eruptions was trapped in shadow, that could account for today's ice. [S8]

That is an estimate, not a measurement. But it shows the idea does not need a miracle.

There is another reason this matters. The old story said the giant impact that made the Moon left it bone dry. Chang'e-5 samples suggest the interior holds more water than once thought. [S13] So the Moon may have had water of its own to give.

Suspect four. The Sun.

The solar wind is a constant stream of protons. At the Moon, about two trillion hit each square metre every second. [S1] When protons strike dusty soil, they can make hydroxyl and water in the grains. [S13]

[EVIDENCE: Chang'e-5 soil]

China's Chang'e-5 brought back samples that show it. The grains hold roughly eleven hundred to twenty five hundred parts per million hydrogen. The signature points strongly to the solar wind. [S13]

So the Sun waters the Moon's skin. But does it fill the polar traps?

The review says probably not. If every proton became water, it would take about eight months of solar wind to lay down a single molecular layer. [S1] That sounds slow, but over billions of years it adds up. The real objection is temperature. Making molecular water needs about four hundred kelvin. The cold traps are far colder. [S1]

The review judges the solar wind a negligible source of polar ice. That is a judgment, not a settled fact. Hydrogen from the wind might still migrate to the poles. That is debated. [S1]

Suspect five. Earth.

This one sounds like science fiction. It is a real hypothesis.

For about five days every month, the Moon passes through a long tail of particles streaming away from Earth. [S10]

[EVIDENCE: Kaguya oxygen ions]

Japan's Kaguya orbiter detected oxygen ions at the Moon only while it was inside that tail. The authors concluded that oxygen from Earth's upper atmosphere is blown to the Moon. [S11]

A University of Alaska team went further. Hydrogen and oxygen that escaped from Earth could land on the Moon in those days, and could form water. [S10]

How much? Their upper estimate was thirty five hundred cubic kilometres or more of polar permafrost or subsurface water. [S10]

I want to be careful here. That is a ceiling, not a forecast. The mainstream view still favours other sources. [S10]

A separate Berkeley team reported evidence that Earth's magnetosphere can create water on the lunar surface. [S12] But that is about a thin hydrated surface layer, not the polar ice itself.

Before we name a culprit, two new studies change the timeline.

[EVIDENCE: 2026 Nature Astronomy accumulation study]

In April 2026, a Nature Astronomy study led by the Weizmann Institute, with the University of Colorado Boulder, modelled the supply. [S2][S3][S4] The ice built up slowly, over about three to three point five billion years. Their models argue against a single giant event, such as one huge comet. [S2][S3]

The oldest, darkest cold traps hold the most ice. In the most ancient ones, more than ten percent of the surface is covered. [S2][S3]

And there was a twist. Shackleton crater, the famous one, has been shadowed for about three and a half billion years. But it became a cold trap only about five hundred million years ago. So it may be a weaker target than we assumed. Another crater, Haworth, was flagged as a priority. [S2][S3]

The study narrows where the ice is. It does not name the source. [S2]

[EVIDENCE: ShadowCam survey]

Then there is a sobering result. A University of Hawaii led team used ShadowCam, a NASA camera flying on Korea's Danuri orbiter. It found no widespread surface ice above roughly twenty to thirty percent by weight. That is its detection limit. [S5][S6]

It did see small patches, twenty to fifty metres across, many near young craters. A few showed signatures above ten percent. [S5][S6]

You might picture a frozen lake. At least at the surface, there is none. ShadowCam sees only the top layer. The ice is probably mixed into the dust, in low amounts. Finding it below one percent will need more sensitive instruments. [S5][S6]

One more complication, for dating the ice. A recent report says craters under about one hundred metres wide may lose their ice within a hundred million years. I could only verify it secondhand, so treat it as provisional. [S17]

So who did it?

Here is my honest reading. The evidence does not support a single culprit. Comets may have supplied what we saw at Cabeus. [S9] Asteroids and volcanoes may have supplied much of the rest, early on. [S1] The Sun waters the surface but probably not the traps. [S1] Earth may add a little, or a lot, or nothing. [S10]

A mixed supply is likely. Different sources, in different eras, in different craters. [S1]

Remember the comet disagreement? It is still unresolved. And that is exactly why the isotopes matter.

Heavy hydrogen is the fingerprint. The ratio of heavy to normal hydrogen in water would help tell comets, asteroids and the Sun apart. [S20] Solar wind hydrogen, for one, is very low in heavy hydrogen. [S13] But nobody has measured this ratio in the polar ice itself. [S1][S20]

That takes a lander that can reach the ice and test it. China's Chang'e-7 is designed to look for water ice at the south pole, and it may help. Its launch was postponed in August, and Scientific American reports a delay until 2027. [S18] No new date was confirmed in the sources I found.

The Moon has been keeping this record for billions of years. We just have not read it yet.
