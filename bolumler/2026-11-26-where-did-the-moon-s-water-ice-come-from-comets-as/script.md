# Where Did the Moon's Water Ice Come From?
## Script
The Moon has ice. That part is settled. What nobody can say for sure is whose water it is. [S1]

Five suspects are on the table. Comets. Asteroids. The solar wind. The Moon's own volcanoes. And, strangest of all, our own planet. [S1][S10]

Today we weigh each one. And we will see why a mission that was meant to launch this August is still on the ground. [S18]

First, the scene of the crime.

The Moon barely tilts on its axis. Because of that, the floors of some craters near the poles never see the Sun. [S1] These are permanently shadowed regions. The coldest of them are cold traps, at about one hundred and ten kelvin or colder. That is minus one hundred and sixty three degrees Celsius. [S1]

All cold traps sit in shadow. But not every shadow is a cold trap. [S1] Together they cover about forty thousand square kilometres. That is a little less than the area of Switzerland. [S16]

[EVIDENCE: LCROSS plume]

In 2009, NASA's LCROSS probe crashed a spent rocket stage into the crater Cabeus. The plume held about five point six percent water by mass, give or take almost three. Pure ice crystals were seen in the debris. [S1][S16]

So the ice is real. The question is how it got there.

Suspect one. Comets.

A comet is a dirty snowball. It looks like the obvious answer. And in 2022, a team led by Mandt analysed the elements in the LCROSS plume. [S9]

[EVIDENCE: LCROSS elemental analysis]

They found that the volatiles were not volcanic. They were most likely cometary. Volcanic and chondritic sources gave the right sulfur, but not enough hydrogen and nitrogen. [S9]

That sounds like a case closed. But it is not.

A 2025 review of lunar ice theories reaches a different view. Comets hit the Moon very fast. A fast impact keeps less water. Slower hits by hydrated asteroids and meteoroids can deliver and retain more. [S1] So the review treats comets as a minor source. [S1]

Two serious readings of the same data. Keep that tension in mind.

Suspect two. The Moon itself.

Volcanism on the Moon peaked roughly four to two billion years ago. Eruptions vented gas, and some of it was water. [S1] The same review calls outgassing and impacts the largest sources of polar ice. Both peaked early. [S1]

[EVIDENCE: volcanic trapping estimate]

How much would it take? NASA slides by Needham and Kring estimate something small. If only about one tenth of one percent of the water vented in the big mare eruptions was trapped in shadow, that could account for today's ice. [S8]

That is an estimate, not a measurement. But it shows the idea does not need a miracle.

There is another reason this matters. The old story said the Moon was left bone dry by the giant impact that made it. The interior now looks wetter than once thought. [S1] So the Moon may have had water of its own to give.

Suspect three. The Sun.

The solar wind is a constant stream of protons. At the Moon, about two trillion hit each square metre every second. [S1] When protons strike dusty soil, they can make hydroxyl and water in the grains. [S13]

[EVIDENCE: Chang'e-5 soil]

China's Chang'e-5 brought back samples that show it. The grains hold between about eleven hundred and twenty five hundred parts per million hydrogen. The signature points strongly to the solar wind. [S13]

So the Sun waters the Moon's skin. But does it fill the polar traps?

The review says probably not. Making molecular water needs about four hundred kelvin. The cold traps are far colder. [S1] And the numbers are thin. About one and a half protons land on each square nanometre in a lunar day. A single layer of water needs about ten molecules there. So even if every proton became water, it would take roughly eight months of solar wind to build one layer. [S1]

The review judges the solar wind a negligible source of polar ice. But this is a judgment, not a settled fact. Hydrogen from the wind might still migrate to the poles. That is debated. [S1]

There is a related idea. Energetic particles might break down minerals inside the shadows and make water on the spot. It has been proposed. It has not been established. [S1]

Suspect four. Earth.

This one sounds like science fiction. It is a real hypothesis.

[EVIDENCE: Kaguya oxygen ions]

The Japanese Kaguya orbiter saw oxygen ions at the Moon only while the Moon was inside Earth's plasma sheet. The authors concluded that oxygen from Earth's upper atmosphere is blown to the Moon by the magnetosphere. [S11]

Then a University of Alaska team went further. Each orbit, the Moon crosses Earth's magnetic tail for about five days. Hydrogen and oxygen that escaped from Earth could land on the Moon in that time, and could form water. [S10]

How much? Their upper estimate was thirty five hundred cubic kilometres or more of polar permafrost or subsurface water. [S10]

I want to be careful here. That is a ceiling, not a forecast. The mainstream view still favours other sources. [S10]

Still, a separate team at Berkeley reported evidence that Earth's magnetosphere can create water on the lunar surface. [S12]

Now, when did the ice arrive?

[EVIDENCE: 2026 Nature Astronomy accumulation study]

In April 2026, a Nature Astronomy study led by Aharonson at the Weizmann Institute, with Hayne at the University of Colorado Boulder, modelled the supply. [S2][S3][S4] The ice built up slowly, over about three to three point five billion years. It was not one giant event. A single huge comet is ruled out. [S2][S3]

Three and a half billion years is about three quarters of the age of the Earth-Moon system. [S1]

The oldest, darkest cold traps hold the most ice. More than ten percent of their surface is covered. [S2][S3]

And there was a twist. Shackleton crater, the famous one, has been shadowed for about three and a half billion years. But it became a cold trap only about five hundred million years ago. So it may be a weaker target than we assumed. Haworth crater was flagged as a priority. [S2][S3]

The study narrows where the ice is. It does not name the source. [S2]

[EVIDENCE: ShadowCam survey]

Then there is a sobering result. A University of Hawaii led team used ShadowCam, a NASA camera flying on Korea's Danuri orbiter. It found no widespread surface ice above roughly twenty to thirty percent by weight. That is the detection limit. [S5][S6]

It did see small patches, twenty to fifty metres across. Many were near young craters. [S5][S6]

So forget the frozen lake. The ice is probably mixed into the dust, in low amounts. Finding it below one percent will need more sensitive instruments. [S5][S6]

So who did it?

Here is my honest reading. The evidence does not support a single culprit. Comets may have supplied the volatiles seen at Cabeus. [S9] Asteroids and volcanoes may have supplied much of the rest, early on. [S1] The Sun waters the surface but probably not the traps. [S1] Earth may add a little, or a lot, or nothing. [S10]

A mixed supply is likely. Different sources, in different eras, in different craters. [S1]

There is a way to break the tie. It is isotopes. The ratio of heavy to normal hydrogen in water differs between comets, asteroids and the Sun. [S20] But nobody has measured it in the polar ice itself. [S1]

That takes a lander, or a rover, that can dig and test the ice. China's Chang'e-7 was built to head for the south pole to look for water ice. [S18] Its launch was postponed in August, and some outlets say 2027. Check the space agency for the current status. [S18]

One more caution. The ice is not simply waiting to be scooped up. Craters under about one hundred metres wide may vanish within a hundred million years, so ice in small craters would have to be recent. That claim comes from a late 2026 report that I could only find secondhand. Treat it as provisional. [S17]

So here is the question I leave you with. If the Moon kept a record of every source in its shadows, which one would you expect to read first?
